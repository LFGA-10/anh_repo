A. Direct Observation
98

Direct observation involves watching the animal in its natural environment
without interference. This method allows caregivers or researchers to gather
real-time data on the animal’s behavior. Observers should note various
behaviors such as playfulness, aggression, social interactions with humans or
other animals, and signs of stress or discomfort. The goal is to create a
comprehensive picture of the animal’s typical behavior patterns.
B. Comparison to Baseline
Establishing a baseline is crucial for effective monitoring. A baseline refers to
the normal range of behaviors exhibited by an animal under typical conditions.
By comparing current observations against this baseline, caregivers can
identify deviations that may indicate health issues or behavioral problems.
Track Activity Level: Monitoring an animal’s activity level involves assessing
how much time it spends being active versus resting. Changes in activity levels
can signal various issues; for example, increased lethargy may indicate illness
while hyperactivity could suggest anxiety or other behavioral concerns.
Response to Approach and Stimulus: Observing how an animal reacts when
approached by humans or exposed to different stimuli (such as noises or new
objects) provides insight into its comfort level and socialization skills. A fearful
response may indicate past trauma or insufficient socialization.

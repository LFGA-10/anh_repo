3. Closed Examination
Following the distant examination, a closed examination is performed where
the veterinarian conducts a physical examination of the animal. This includes
117

palpating various body parts to check for abnormalities such as swelling or
pain and examining specific areas like ears, eyes, mouth, skin, and abdomen
for signs of disease. The veterinarian may also assess neurological function by
checking reflexes and responses to stimuli.
4. Taking Vital Signs
Vital signs are critical indicators of an animal’s health status. During this step,
veterinarians measure parameters such as temperature (normal range: 100-
102.5°F), heart rate (normal range varies by species), respiratory rate (normal
range varies by species), and capillary refill time (to assess circulation).
Abnormal vital signs can indicate underlying health issues that require further
investigation.
5. Taking Samples
If initial examinations suggest specific health concerns, veterinarians may take
samples for further analysis.
Common samples include blood (for complete blood counts or biochemical
profiles), urine (for urinalysis), feces (for parasitology), skin scrapings (for
dermatological conditions), and swabs from lesions or discharge sites (to
identify infections). These samples are essential for confirming diagnoses and
guiding treatment decisions.
6. Analysis of Laboratory Results
Once samples are collected, they are sent to a laboratory for analysis. The
results provide critical information regarding organ function, presence of
infections or parasites, electrolyte imbalances, and other health indicators.
Veterinarians interpret these results in conjunction with clinical findings to
arrive at a definitive diagnosis.

After the nurse crop is harvested remove stovers or straws and cut back the weeds using a panga or slasher.

During the establishment year, grazing is not recommended to avoid grass still with weak roots being uprooted by the livestock.

Vegetative propagation

Pasture establishment is faster when starting from seeds but vegetative propagation (common in fodder crops) can be used where there is no regular supply of pasture seeds. Species that can be vegetatively propagated include Bracharia spp., Chloris Guyana, Desmodium spp. and Stylosanthes spp. Root splits of the chosen species should be planted into cultivated land and made firm. If unrooted stolons are being used, these should have at least 3 active bud sites each.

The disadvantages of this method are that it is labour intensive; the pasture takes longer to establish i.e. forming a complete soil cover. Susceptibility of seedlings to pests and weeds is higher with vegetative establishment; however hazards of soil erosion are overcome by not preparing a fine seedbed.

4.5.7. Management after sowing

 For proper management the following should be respected:

Weeding should be done as much as possible

In the establishment year grasses reach the early flowering stage 3–4 months after planting. At this stage the plant is not firmly anchored in the soil and therefore it is usually advisable to make hay rather than graze the pastures to avoid the risk of the cattle pulling out the young shoots.

If the pasture must be grazed during the establishment year, grazing should be light enough (use calves) to allow the plants to establish firmly in the soil.

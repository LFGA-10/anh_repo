D. Antitussives and expectorant

			i. 	Antitussives agents

They diminish the frequency of coughing and should not be used to suppress productive coughs. They can be either locally acting (e.g. glycerin), or centrally acting (e.g. opiates); they can be divided into opioid and non-opioid antitussives.

Butorphanol and codeine are potent opioid antitussives but may cause sedation, ataxia and excessive respiratory depression.

Dextromethorphan is almost as effective a cough suppressant as codeine but does not cause respiratory depression.

Diphenhydramine has anticholinergic, antihistamine and antitussive properties. Its antitussive mechanism of action remains unclear.

			ii. 	Expectorants

Expectorants theoretically make the bronchiole secretions less viscous but their efficacy is questionable.

Potassium iodide is an oral saline expectorant, which causes irritation to thegastric mucosa that in turn increases bronchiole secretion through a vagal reflex.

Guaifenesin may stimulate the gastric mucosa and increase respiratory tract secretions via reflex.

e. Bronchodilator

Introduction

Bronchodilators are a type of medication that makes breathing easier by relaxing the muscles in the lungs and widening the airways (bronchi).

They're often used to treat long-term conditions where the airways may become narrow and inflamed

Bronchodilators may be either:

short-acting – used as short-term relief from sudden, unexpected attacks of breathlessness

long-acting – used regularly, to help control breathlessness

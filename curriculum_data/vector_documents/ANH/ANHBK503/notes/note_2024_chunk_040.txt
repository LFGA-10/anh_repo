Beeswax melts at temperatures between 63 and 65 °C. Overheated or burnt
wax is worthless. Wax that has been melted in iron, copper or zinc pots loses
its smell and color. Slow heating over a long period has the same effect. The
quality of beeswax is judged from its color and purity. Light wax has the
highest value.

Old combs are dark and must be processed separately, but they yield very little
wax and are hardly worth the trouble. The wax must be washed before it is
melted. Put the combs in clean water. Do not use tap water if you know that it
is very alkaline, or add a tablespoon of vinegar to every liter of water. The wax
must be melted down to purify it further. Of the various possible methods of
doing this, only the straining method and the solar wax extractor will be

2. Pollen
When the bees are collecting a lot of pollen you can use a pollen trap, placed in
front of the flight entrance, to collect the pollen pellets. After a few days, move
the trap to another hive so that the colonies are not deprived of too much of
their protein supply. The pollen trap is a simple box open at the front and
back.
Alternatively, beekeepers may use pollen traps that are placed inside the hive,
which encourage bees to pass through a mesh screen that collects pollen
pellets as they enter the hive. Once collected, bee pollen is carefully cleaned,
dried, and stored in airtight containers for consumption or sale.
3. Royal Jelly
Royal jelly is produced by worker bees and fed to queen larvae during their
development. To harvest royal jelly, beekeepers must first identify queen cells
within the hive where royal jelly is being produced and fed to the developing
queen larvae.

Establishment of New Colony: The new colony starts with the queen laying
eggs, and the worker bees building comb and foraging for food. Over time, the
colony grows and develops, repeating the cycle of swarming as conditions
become favorable for reproduction.
Cause of Swarming
➢ Overcrowding: The population has grown too large and the hive is too
small to accommodate all the bees.
➢ Queen Cell Production: When the colony senses that the current
queen's pheromone levels are declining, they may begin raising new
queen bees by constructing queen cells and feeding selected larvae with
royal jelly. The presence of queen cells can signal to the colony that it is
time to prepare for swarming.

➢ Environmental Factors: External factors such as temperature,
humidity, and weather conditions can also influence swarming. Warm,
sunny days with mild temperatures are ideal for swarming, as bees are
more likely to venture outside the hive and scout for new nesting sites.
➢ Genetic Predisposition: Some honeybee colonies may have a genetic
predisposition to swarm more frequently than others. Certain bee breeds
or strains may exhibit swarming behavior as part of their natural
reproductive cycle.
➢ Disease or Parasites: Health issues such as disease or infestations by
parasites like Varroa mites can weaken a colony and disrupt the normal
hive dynamics, potentially triggering swarming as a response to stress.
2.2.5 Supersedure
Supersedure is the bees’ way of replacing an existing queen with a new queen
in the same hive, without intervention by the beekeeper. Supersedure is the
natural way of requeening. After the supersedure has taken place, the old
queen dies.
